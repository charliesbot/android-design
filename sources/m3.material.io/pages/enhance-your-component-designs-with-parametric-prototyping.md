_Parametric prototyping can take your design explorations from scattered to structured_

When it comes to creating new components, like Material’s Expressive time picker, keeping track of design iterations and feedback in traditional UI software can be a real struggle. You’re often left with a design file that’s messy and inconsistent. Who hasn’t dealt with disorganized layers, broken components, or disjointed sketches after weeks of design reviews?

**Parametric prototyping makes the design process dynamic and efficient.** When you use interactive prototypes with flexible parameters instead of static drawings, you can visualize different design iterations in real time and easily refine ideas over the course of a project’s lifecycle.

#### From scattered to structured

The design process in tools like Figma or Illustrator often resembles a plot of scattered dots: Frames spread out everywhere. A massive grid of instances. Zooming around the canvas to find the right example.

**Prototyping with AI tools and using interactive sliders is a powerful way to visualize design changes on the fly.** Rather than fixating on a single setting and losing track of it within a sprawling design file, you can build a prototype that decouples variables such as color ranges, padding amounts, corner radius, or type size in code.

Using this approach provides a focused yet flexible space for you to explore. You can add new elements and immediately customize them in your prototype’s settings panel as you continue to refine the design.

#### When is it worth the effort?

Parametric prototyping works best for:
- Complex components that support a range of interactions, like Material time pickers, toolbars, or tabs.
- Minor system updates, to compare old and new versions side-by-side.

#### How to optimize your prototype settings

As you add and tune new sliders, your control and confidence in a component design will continue to grow. Here are some best practices for getting the most out of your prototypes:

[VIDEO] A chart of dots with sliders on the X and Y axes

_**1. Map the sweet spots:** Plot out an organized grid of combinations to find the ideal combination of parameters for your design. By clearly defining boundaries, you can document how other creators are experimenting with the component and what areas should be off-limits to keep the design cohesive._

[VIDEO] Section headers for design variables (color settings, size and spacing, and typography)

_**2. Fine-tune your parameters:** The system scales with each new idea. Every time a new design parameter is identified and added, the prototype becomes a more powerful tool to explore how parameters interact or conflict with each other._

[VIDEO] A malleable rectangle with a set of slider components underneath to control parameters

_**3. Group your parameters:** Create rules that tether certain parameters together so you can avoid undesired configurations and guide more considered solutions._

[VIDEO] A time picker component with a preset toggle

_**4. Turn designs into specs:** By introducing overlay toggles for keylines, color tokens, and spatial measurements, prototypes can double as an interactive spec document that bridges the gap between design and engineering._

[VIDEO] A list of preset buttons for prototyping

_**5. Create presets to save what works:** Save your favorite design combinations as quick presets, like adding a button to export your current settings as a JSON file to add to a subsequent prompt._

[VIDEO] A button that says “randomize parameters”

_**6. Randomize to break patterns:** Using a "randomize" button can help diverge from human bias, find unexpected visual wins, and stress-test edge cases._

#### A resilient design system

By moving from scattered static mocks to an organized parametric prototype, you can efficiently calibrate components and always have an accurate source of truth. Adding this approach to your toolkit will ultimately reinvigorate and streamline the design process for you and your team.
